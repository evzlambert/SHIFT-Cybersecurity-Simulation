# Cybersecurity Breach Simulation — Structured Debrief Guide

## SHIFT Program — Saint-Etienne, France

**Duration:** 2 hours (120 minutes)
**Format:** Instructor-led group discussion with team sharing, cross-team comparison, and individual reflection
**Timing:** Conducted as a separate session after the 3-hour simulation
**Preparation:** Teams should have their exported JSON reports and physical station deliverables (paper forms, wristbands, handoff templates, press statements, etc.)

---

## Debrief Principles

Before you begin, ground the session in these facilitation principles:

- **No Monday-morning quarterbacking.** Decisions made under time pressure with incomplete information are not wrong because better information emerged later. Honor the conditions under which choices were made.
- **Trade-offs, not right answers.** Every decision in this simulation had costs. The debrief explores those costs, not whether teams "got it right."
- **Discipline matters.** Students from different backgrounds saw different things. Surface those differences — they are the learning.
- **Emotion is data.** If students felt frustrated, overwhelmed, or conflicted during the simulation, that IS the experience of crisis leadership. Name it.

---

| Block | Time | Duration | Focus |

## Block 1: Emotional Decompression (10 min)

**Purpose:** Release the emotional residue of the simulation before analytical discussion begins.

**Instructor says:**
> "Before we analyze anything, I want to hear how that felt. Not what you decided — how it felt to be in that room making those decisions."

**Go-around prompt (one sentence per person, popcorn style):**
> "In one sentence: what was the most stressful moment for you personally?"

**Follow-up questions (choose 2-3):**
- "Raise your hand if there was a moment you felt genuinely overwhelmed." *(Normalize it — most hands should go up.)*
- "Did anyone feel tension within their team? What was it about?"
- "Was there a moment where your real-world professional instinct kicked in — where you stopped role-playing and started reacting as yourself?"
- "How did it feel to make decisions with incomplete information and a ticking clock?"

**Instructor note:** This block is short but essential. Students who are still emotionally activated will not engage analytically. Let them exhale first. Validate that the stress was intentional and mirrors real crisis leadership.

---

## Block 2: Station Artifact Gallery Walk (15 min)

**Purpose:** Teams physically compare the artifacts they created at action stations. This is the most kinesthetic part of the debrief — students move around the room.

**Setup:** Each team lays out their physical deliverables on their table:
- Paper MAR form (Station 1)
- Staff communication bulletin (Station 2)
- Patient ID wristband (Station 3)
- Shift handoff template (Station 4)
- Paper patient record (Station 5)
- Press statement (Station 6)
- Family information sheet (Station 7)
- Board recommendation memo (Station 8)

**Instructor says:**
> "Stand up. You have 8 minutes to walk to other teams' tables and look at what they created. Pay attention to what they included that you didn't — and what you included that they missed."

**After the walk (7 min discussion):**

1. **MAR Forms:** "Hold up your MAR forms. How many teams included a second verifier signature? How many included an allergy alert in a distinct color? Who included a patient photo space?" *(Highlight variation — there is no standard because the standard was in the encrypted system.)*

2. **Patient Wristbands:** "Who solved the 14-Martins problem differently? What was the most creative solution you saw?"

3. **Press Statements:** "I'd like two teams to read their opening sentence aloud." *(Compare tone, specificity, and what was omitted.)*

4. **Family Information Sheets:** "Which team's sheet would actually reassure you if your parent were in this hospital? Why?"

**Key teaching point:**
> "Look at the variation across teams. In a real hospital during a real breach, this variation happens across units, across shifts, across departments — simultaneously. There is no standard because the standard was electronic. This is why downtime preparedness matters BEFORE the crisis."

---

## Block 3: Decision Comparison — The Breach (15 min)

**Purpose:** Surface different approaches to the initial crisis and explore trade-offs.

**Instructor preparation:** Before this block, quickly scan the team reports (or ask teams verbally) to identify which options each team chose for Phase 1 and 2 decisions.

### Phase 1 Decisions

**First Priority Action (p1_priority):**
> "Show of hands: who prioritized downtime procedures first? Who prioritized network isolation? Who split the team? Who called a huddle?"

Discussion questions:
- "Teams who isolated the network first: your backup servers are clean. Teams who activated downtime first: your nurses had forms 20 minutes earlier. Which mattered more — and to whom?"
- "Is this a clinical decision or a technical decision? Who should make it?"

**Staff Communication (p1_communication):**
> "How did you communicate to 1,200 staff? What happened as a result?"

- "Teams who used the PA: patients heard it. Teams who used runners: staff posted on social media before bulletins arrived. Teams who used WhatsApp: a journalist has a screenshot. Every method had a cost. Which cost was most acceptable to you?"

**Patient Safety Strategy (p1_safety):**
- "Who canceled all surgeries? Who continued all surgeries? Why?"
- "For the teams that continued: did the specimen labeling error surprise you? For the teams that canceled: the bowel obstruction patient was transferred 45 minutes away. Is that acceptable?"

### Phase 2 Decisions (select 2)

**Medication Safety:**
- "After the near-miss — what did you do? Who implemented buddy-system checks? What did that cost you in staffing?"
- "The wristband solution (Station 3) some teams created — did that match the decision you made in the app? Or did you have a different approach?"

**Paper Records:**
- "You just experienced writing a paper patient record at Station 5. How long did it take? Now multiply that by 287 inpatients, 24 hours, multiple encounters per patient. That is the back-entry problem."

---

## Block 4: Decision Comparison — The Pressure (15 min)

**Purpose:** Explore stakeholder management, communication under pressure, and ethical complexity.

### Media Response

> "Two teams: read your press statement opening sentence." *(If not already done in Block 2.)*

- "Who held a press conference? Who issued a written statement only? What was the public perception?"
- "The journalist asked about the ransom. How did you handle that? Is a non-answer an answer?"

### Family Confrontation

- "Who sent the Incident Commander? Who sent the CNO to the bedside? Who sent a patient advocate?"
- "The man in the lobby represents hundreds of families. Your answer to him becomes your template. Did you think about scalability when you made that decision?"

### Staff Morale

> "Marie-Claire's voicemail. Raise your hand if that moment hit you emotionally."

- "What did you do for your staff? Was it enough? What was the cost?"
- "Is staff morale a patient safety issue? At what point does staff fatigue become a clinical risk that requires intervention — not just support?"

### Social Media

- "Who tried to get the nurse's relative to take down the post? What happened?"
- "This is a real dilemma: an employee's family member posted information that is mostly accurate. You cannot control it without damaging staff trust. You cannot ignore it without losing the public narrative. What is the right move?"

### The Surgery Conflict

> "The Chief of Surgery says: 'We operated for decades before computers.' The HIM Director says: 'And we had higher error rates.' Who is right?"

- "This is a governance question. Who has the authority to decide whether elective surgery resumes during downtime? Is it a clinical decision? A data integrity decision? An executive decision?"
- "How did your team resolve this? Was the Incident Commander the tiebreaker?"

---

## Block 5: The Three Hardest Decisions (20 min)

**Purpose:** Deep ethical and strategic analysis of the simulation's most consequential decisions.

### The Ransom (8 min)

> "Show of hands: who recommended paying? Who recommended refusing? Who negotiated?"

**Structured debate format:**
1. Ask one "pay" team to present their reasoning (2 min)
2. Ask one "refuse" team to present their reasoning (2 min)
3. Open the floor for cross-team questions (4 min)

Key prompts:
- "For the teams who refused: 40,000 patient records were published, including 1,200 mental health records and 340 HIV status records. Real people are harmed. Can you defend that?"
- "For the teams who paid: you funded a criminal organization. The decryption key worked for 80% of systems. You can never confirm the data was deleted. Can you defend that?"
- "Is this a financial decision, an ethical decision, a clinical decision, or a legal decision? Who should make it?"
- "What does Loi LOPMI (2023) require if you pay? Does that change your calculus?"
- "ANSSI says don't pay. But ANSSI doesn't have 180,000 patients whose HIV status may be published. How do you weigh government guidance against patient harm?"

### GDPR Notification (6 min)

- "Who filed the preliminary notification immediately? Who waited until the deadline?"
- "The team that notified all 180,000 patients simultaneously: what happened to the domestic violence survivor whose address was in the system? Did you anticipate that?"
- "What goes in a CNIL notification when you don't know the full scope? How do you describe uncertainty to a regulator without creating legal liability?"
- "Teams that completed the CNIL station (Station 9): what did you list under 'unknown'? Was it honest?"

### System Restoration (6 min)

- "Who chose the backup restore? Who chose the hybrid? Who chose decrypt-and-clean? Who chose the new clean instance?"
- "Every option had a data integrity cost. Option A loses 48 hours of pre-breach data. Option C starts with an empty EHR. Option D costs €120,000 and takes 5 weeks to reconcile."
- "Who decides the restoration strategy? Is this a technical decision? A clinical decision? A financial decision? A data integrity decision? The answer is: all of the above. How does your organization make decisions that are all of the above?"

## Block 6: Cross-Disciplinary Perspectives (15 min)

**Purpose:** Surface how professional discipline shaped what students noticed, prioritized, and recommended.

**Instructor says:**
> "I want to hear from specific disciplines now. Not your team's decision — YOUR perspective, from your background."

### For Health Sciences / Clinical Students:
- "What did you see that the non-clinical students didn't? What patient safety risks kept you up at night during the simulation?"
- "The five rights of medication administration — name, drug, dose, route, time. How many of those could you reliably verify during paper downtime?"
- "A nurse calculated a dose by hand and made a math error. This is real — electronic systems do this calculation automatically. What is the clinical impact of losing decision support?"

### For Healthcare Administration Students:
- "Walk us through the financial cascade. Revenue loss, overtime, back-entry staffing, insurance, potential GDPR fines, legal costs, reputation damage. What's the total estimate?"
- "The board memo (Station 8): was that a document you felt comfortable writing? What would you change?"
- "Governance during a crisis: who actually has authority? The Incident Commander? The CEO? The Chief Medical Officer? The DPO? How do you resolve competing authorities?"

### For Health Informatics & Information Management Students:
- "The 11% unmatched records. The 34% missing DOB. The 18% illegible. You lived this when you created the paper patient record (Station 5). What does this mean for data integrity when systems come back?"
- "Back-entry (Station 10): what was your priority order? How did you handle quality assurance?"
- "The coding and billing backlog: when does this become a financial crisis layered on top of an operational crisis?"

### For Graduate Students:
- "You had the broadest view. What did you see about organizational resilience that the undergraduates might have missed?"
- "If you were advising a hospital CEO on cybersecurity preparedness investment, what would your top three recommendations be — based on what you just experienced?"

**Key teaching point:**
> "Notice that each discipline saw a different crisis. The clinical students saw a patient safety crisis. The admin students saw a financial and governance crisis. The HIM students saw a data integrity crisis. The graduate students saw a leadership crisis. A real hospital cyberattack is ALL of these simultaneously. That is why interdisciplinary preparedness matters."

## Block 7: Real-World Connections (10 min)

**Purpose:** Ground the simulation in actual events to reinforce that this is not hypothetical.

**Instructor presents these briefly (1-2 min each) and asks students to connect to their simulation experience:**

### Centre Hospitalier de Corbeil-Essonnes (2022)
- Ransomware attack, ransom demanded, hospital refused to pay
- 11 GB of patient data published on the dark web
- *"Your simulation included this exact scenario. How does knowing it really happened change how you feel about your ransom decision?"*

### Centre Hospitalier de Versailles (2022)
- Forced patient transfers, months of disruption
- Staff worked on paper for extended periods
- *"You experienced paper processes for a simulated 72 hours. Versailles staff did it for months. What breaks first — the processes or the people?"*

### WannaCry / NHS (2017)
- 80 hospital trusts affected, 19,000 appointments canceled, £92M cost
- A single unpatched vulnerability
- *"The Sainte-Claire attack started with a phishing email 11 days before encryption. What does that tell you about the difference between preventing an attack and detecting one?"*

### CommonSpirit Health (2022)
- 140 US hospitals, weeks of EHR downtime
- *"Scale this: you managed one 350-bed hospital. Imagine 140 hospitals simultaneously."*

### ANSSI Statistics
- France: 400% increase in ransomware attacks 2020-2022
- Healthcare is the 3rd most targeted sector
- *"This is not a question of IF but WHEN. What did this simulation teach you about organizational preparedness?"*

## Block 8: Lessons & Preparedness (10 min)

**Purpose:** Transition from analysis to application — what would they do to prepare?

**Rapid-fire team exercise (5 min):**
> "Each team: you have 3 minutes. Based on everything you experienced, write down your team's top 5 recommendations for hospital cybersecurity preparedness. These should be actionable — not 'be more prepared.' Specific."

**Share-out (5 min):**
Each team reads their #1 recommendation. Instructor captures on the board. Look for patterns:
- How many teams recommend downtime drills?
- How many recommend pre-printed forms?
- How many recommend communication plans?
- How many recommend staff training?
- How many recommend technical controls (patching, backups, segmentation)?

**Key teaching point:**
> "Notice the balance between technical and human recommendations. Cybersecurity in healthcare is not an IT problem. It is an organizational resilience problem that requires clinical, administrative, informatics, legal, and communication preparedness. That is why YOU — regardless of your discipline — have a role in this."

---

## Block 9: Individual Written Reflection (10 min)

**Purpose:** Personal synthesis — move from group discussion to individual meaning-making.

**Distribute or display these prompts. Students write individually — these can be collected as an assessment artifact.**

1. **The Moment.** Describe one specific moment during the simulation when you felt the weight of the decision. What made it difficult? What did you ultimately decide, and why? *(3-4 sentences)*

2. **The Discipline.** How did your professional background (health sciences, healthcare administration, HIM, informatics) shape what you prioritized? What would you have missed without your teammates from other disciplines? *(3-4 sentences)*

3. **The Gap.** What is one thing you now believe every healthcare organization should have in place BEFORE a cyberattack — that most probably don't? *(2-3 sentences)*

4. **The Transfer.** How will this simulation experience influence your professional practice — regardless of what role you eventually hold in healthcare? *(2-3 sentences)*

5. **The Question.** What is one question you still have — something the simulation raised but didn't fully resolve for you? *(1-2 sentences)*

**Instructor says to close:**
> "The systems come back in days. The trust takes months. The lessons last careers. Take yours with you."

## Instructor Notes

### Adapting for Time
- **Running short (90 min available):** Cut Block 2 to 8 min (skip the walk, do verbal comparison), cut Block 7 to 5 min (present 2 cases only), reduce Block 9 to 5 min (prompts 1 and 4 only)
- **Running long (extra time):** Expand Block 5 to 30 min (add structured debate format for GDPR), expand Block 6 to 20 min (role-swap exercise: ask clinical students to argue the HIM position and vice versa)

### Common Student Reactions
- **"We should have paid the ransom"** — Push on: what precedent does that set? Can you verify data deletion? Are you funding the next attack on another hospital?
- **"We should never pay"** — Push on: 1,200 mental health records and 340 HIV status records are now public. Real people are harmed. Is ideological purity worth that?
- **"This wouldn't happen at a well-funded hospital"** — Challenge directly: WannaCry hit the NHS. CommonSpirit is a $35B system. Versailles is a well-resourced French hospital. Funding helps but does not prevent.
- **"IT should have prevented this"** — This is the most important misconception to address. The phishing email that started the attack was sent to a radiology technician. Prevention requires organizational culture, not just technical controls.

### Connecting to Other SHIFT Modules
Use these bridges if students are also completing other SHIFT activities:

| Module | Debrief Connection |
|--------|--------------------|
| **Project Management** | "The recovery phase IS a project. What's the scope? The timeline? The budget? The stakeholders? The risks? You just lived a project initiation." |
| **Interoperability** | "Every cascading failure — PACS to radiology to surgery — was an interoperability dependency. Interoperability is a strength AND an attack surface." |
| **Data Analytics** | "When systems come back, your analytics will include manually entered crisis data. 34% missing DOB. 18% illegible. How do you trust population health analytics built on that foundation?" |
| **Ethics** | "The ransom debate. The GDPR notification to the DV survivor. The social media post from the nurse's family. Every one of those is an ethics case study." |
| **Hackathon** | "Your preparedness recommendations from Block 8 — those are hackathon project seeds. Which one could you build?" |

### Assessment Integration
The following artifacts can be collected and assessed using the rubric in the Facilitation Guide:
- Team JSON export from the student app (decisions, consequences, station deliverables, after-action summary)
- Physical station artifacts (MAR forms, wristbands, handoff templates, press statements, family sheets, board memos)
- Individual written reflection from Block 9
- Instructor observation notes from simulation and debrief participation
